Work

I mostly work on HIV epidemic spreading in Sub-Saharan Africa, mainly on the quantitative analysis of the data from the HPTN01 (PopART) trial , within the PANGEA consortium. My research focuses on understanding how sexual partnership networks in sub-Saharan Africa are shaping the dynamics of HIV spreading, and how to tailor public policy interventions aimed at reducing the burden of HIV on the population based on this knowledge; for example, what is the role of the hard-to-reach individuals who may have a high number of sexual partners, and therefore may be at high risk of acquiring/transmitting HIV? How much would a strategy focused on this sub-population reduce the overall incidence, compared to different choices? Answering such questions involves working with big data*, developing/maintaining epidemic models**, and collaborating with other members of the statistics and modelling team, as well as researchers around the World. I am also trying to bring in information gathered from Phylogenetic analysis into epidemic models, a task that is challenging but necessary, as recent advances in the field are giving us an unprecedented flux of high-quality data that are of paramount importance in reconstructing the transmission dynamics. Occasionally, I collaborate with other researchers on other diseases related projects, mainly COVID-19.

*In particular, sexual behaviour surveys and health-related data

**I am one of the main developers of PopART-IBM.

Publications

Below are short summaries of my publications from the past three years. For a full list, visit my Google Scholar page.

Biased estimates of phylogenetic branch lengths from the discretised Gamma model (2026)

Systematic Biology — L. Ferretti, T. Golubchik, F. Di Lauro, M. Ghafari, J. Villabona-Arenas, et al.

The discrete Gamma model (DGM) is a standard tool in phylogenetics for modelling variation in substitution rates across genomic sites. This paper demonstrates that the DGM systematically overestimates branch lengths, with the bias growing as more sequences are added to the alignment. The finding has broad implications for molecular clock analyses and any downstream inference that relies on accurate branch-length estimates from large datasets.

doi:10.1093/sysbio/syag037

Large connected components in sexual networks and HIV transmission in Sub-Saharan Africa (2025)

Journal of Theoretical Biology — F. Di Lauro, W. J. M. Probert, M. Pickles, A. Cori, R. Hinch, L. Ferretti, et al.

Using data from the HPTN 071 (PopART) trial and the individual-based model PopART-IBM, this study characterises the structure of sexual partnership networks in high-HIV-burden communities in Zambia and South Africa. A single giant connected component typically encompassing over 40% of the population — concentrated among young adults aged 20–40 — is identified at each site, and more than 80% of all simulated HIV transmissions occur within it. The results argue for prevention strategies that prioritise highly sexually active young individuals.

View preprint / repository record

Clade I mpox vaccination: strategies for deployment and evaluation (2025)

EBioMedicine 119 — C. Pley, J. Panovska-Griffiths, R. Hinch, C. Wymant, L. Abeler-Dörner, et al.

Clade I mpox continues to spread in Central Africa with no sign of abating. While the MVA-BN vaccine showed 82% effectiveness against Clade IIb, its protection against Clade I is unknown. This perspective argues for cohort studies — ideally nested within existing HIV/STI cohorts in high-transmission areas — as the most rigorous design to generate urgently needed effectiveness data. Children receive special attention given their disproportionate disease burden and higher case-fatality rates.

doi:10.1016/j.ebiom.2025.105890

Bayesian mixture models for phylogenetic source attribution (2025)

Statistical Methods in Medical Research 34(3) — A. Blenkinsop, L. Sofocleous, F. Di Lauro, E. G. Kostaki, A. van Sighem, et al.

Identifying who-infected-whom from pathogen genomic data is complicated by false phylogenetic signals: simulations show that 37% of candidate transmission pairs carry spurious signal. This paper develops Bayesian hierarchical mixture models that jointly leverage consensus sequences and time-since-infection estimates to attribute transmission sources at the population level. Applied to Amsterdam MSM data (2010–2021), the model finds that 30–39-year-olds contributed the largest share of onward HIV transmissions, with Hilbert-space Gaussian process priors achieving the lowest error rates.

doi:10.1177/09622802241309750

Drivers of epidemic dynamics in real time from daily digital COVID-19 measurements (2024)

Science 385(6710), eadm8103 — M. Kendall, L. Ferretti, C. Wymant, D. Tsallis, J. Petrie, A. Di Francia, F. Di Lauro, et al.

Daily data from the NHS COVID-19 app (up to 18 million users in England and Wales) were used to decompose the reproduction number into contact rates and per-contact transmission probability, delivering estimates to health authorities several days ahead of any other surveillance stream. When Omicron emerged, the analysis showed the epidemic was being driven by rising transmissibility rather than increased contact rates — a distinction with direct policy relevance. The dataset also revealed sharp day-of-week effects and spikes during mass-gathering events such as the UEFA Euro 2021 football tournament.

doi:10.1126/science.adm8103

Digital measurement of SARS-CoV-2 transmission risk from 7 million contacts (2024)

Nature 626(7997) — L. Ferretti, C. Wymant, J. Petrie, D. Tsallis, M. Kendall, A. Ledda, F. Di Lauro, et al.

Leveraging Bluetooth proximity data from over 7 million contacts logged by the NHS COVID-19 app, this study provides the largest empirical quantification of SARS-CoV-2 transmission risk as a function of exposure duration, distance, and setting. The results informed the app's alert thresholds in real time and demonstrate how large-scale digital contact tracing can simultaneously reduce transmission and generate fine-grained epidemiological intelligence during an active pandemic.

doi:10.1038/s41586-023-06952-2

Enhancing global preparedness during an ongoing pandemic from partial and noisy data (2023)

PNAS Nexus 2(6), pgad192 — P. P. Klamser, V. d'Andrea, F. Di Lauro, A. Zachariae, S. Bontorin, A. Di Nardo, et al.

This paper introduces an integrated framework that combines genomic surveillance, air-travel mobility data, and epidemic modelling to estimate the pandemic potential of a newly emerging variant while it is still in its early, partially-observed phase. A "pandemic delay" metric quantifies how much time the world has before a variant reaches epidemic scale globally. Validated on six SARS-CoV-2 lineages (Alpha through Omicron sub-variants), the framework achieved 81% accuracy for Alpha and highlighted why phylogenetic, mobility, and epidemiological signals must be analysed jointly rather than in isolation.

doi:10.1093/pnasnexus/pgad192
